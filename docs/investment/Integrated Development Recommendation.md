## 1. The finding that governs everything else

**The South Andrews development program, as presently modelled, does not support the land at any price — including a price of zero.**

This is not a negotiating conclusion. It is arithmetic, and it holds across every scenario tested:

| Scenario | Yield on cost at the $8.0M land input | **Yield on cost at a land basis of $0** |
|---|---|---|
| SA-A — 8-storey maximum fleet | 2.92% | **3.26%** |
| SA-B — 6-storey balanced two-site | 3.14% | **3.62%** |
| SA-C — 4-storey minimum viable | 3.00% | **3.73%** |
| SA-C0 — single-storey staging ground | 2.73% | **4.49%** |

Against a 7.0% institutional planning hurdle (S3 `Assumptions!D66`), **free land does not close the gap in any scheme.** The largest movement free land buys is 176 basis points, on the smallest scheme. The shortfall is structural, in the relationship between South Florida construction cost and the rents this program can plausibly command — not in the acquisition price.

The break-even office rent required to reach 7% on the leading scheme is **$131 per rentable square foot NNN**. The modelled rent is $50. The two current models disagree about whether the right figure is $36 or $50 (OQ-15). Neither argument reaches $131.

This finding is consistent with, and sharpens, the repository's existing canonical position (Project Canon §3, CAN-022): both current models independently show the base project failing its return requirements. This analysis adds the part that was missing — **that the failure is not curable by the land price.**

### Every increment of vertical scale destroys value

| Increment | Additional capital | Additional NOI | Marginal return |
|---|---|---|---|
| SA-C0 → SA-C (1 storey to 4) | +$20,503,726 | +$670,920 | **3.27%** |
| SA-C → SA-B (4 storeys to 6) | +$18,975,902 | +$651,244 | **3.43%** |
| SA-B → SA-A (6 storeys to 8) | +$16,421,998 | +$347,366 | **2.12%** |

No increment earns a cost of capital. The 8-storey increment is the worst, returning 2.12% on $16.4M of additional capital — and on the parking construct S3 itself uses, only **1.23%**. **Scale on this site adds risk without adding return.**

## 2. Recommendation

### 2.1 On acquisition

**Do not commit to acquisition at the $8,000,000 working opening-offer input on the strength of the development program.** The program does not support that basis, and no negotiated reduction within a plausible range would change that. Per D-P1, the $8,000,000 figure is an acquisition-strategy input only — not proof of value, and it does not itself authorise an offer.

> **Amendment 2026-07-29 (D-P13).** The working opening-offer basis is now **$7,500,000** — a tactical negotiation figure, not an economically supported land value, and not authority to submit an offer. **This recommendation is unchanged by that decision.** A $500,000 reduction is a negotiated reduction within a plausible range, and the analysis above is precisely that no such reduction changes the answer: the shortfall persists at a land basis of zero. The figures throughout this document remain computed at the **$8,000,000 modeled basis** (CAN-004a) and have not been restated. D-P13 authorises no offer, and gate **G8** remains open.

Instead, **convert the next 90–120 days into a bounded pre-acquisition diligence programme costing roughly $100,000–$200,000** that can do one of three things: produce evidence that materially changes the answer, support a fundamentally different transaction structure, or support a documented decision not to proceed. Every item on that list can be completed before any capital is committed to land, and two of them could independently end the enquiry.

### 2.2 On what to build, if the project proceeds

**Preferred scenario: SA-C — the 4-storey Minimum Viable Development.**

| | SA-C |
|---|---|
| Program | L1 med-tail 12,000 RSF + lobby + loading + passenger transfer + 10 staging bays + 15 at-grade stalls · L2–L3 structured parking (154 stalls) · L4 clinical/medical office 24,000 GSF (20,400 RSF) |
| Total | 108,000 GSF · 169 stalls · 4 storeys |
| Core all-in cost | **$40,898,910** including land |
| Tenant-ready all-in cost | **$46,763,515** |
| Yield on cost | 3.00% (code-constrained parking) / 3.32% (S3 parking construct) |

**Why SA-C wins on a risk-adjusted basis, despite a marginally lower yield than SA-B:**

1. **It is below the height-review threshold.** Four storeys avoids the discretionary review that governs everything above six (S6 p.37). This removes the largest single entitlement risk from the critical path — and that risk is not priced anywhere in any model.
2. **Its parking argument is the most defensible.** A 1.17× accessory ratio is a building that parks itself with a modest surplus. SA-A's is 1.66×; S8's rejected rental scheme is 4.3×. No source cites any ULDR limit on accessory-parking ratios (CVR-32 / OQ-27), so the lower the ratio, the less the project depends on an uncited argument.
3. **It has a genuine parking surplus.** SA-C carries +25 stalls over the City requirement. **SA-B carries a one-stall deficit** — it has no monetisable parking and no deck capacity for a fleet at all.
4. **It avoids medium-voltage electrical service.** 870 kW managed, 1.1 MVA at 480 V.
5. **$18.98M less capital at risk than SA-B**, for NOI that is only $651,244 lower — a marginal return of 3.43% on the increment that is being avoided.
6. **Only $1,075,000 of genuinely mobility-specific spend** (chargers $300K, conduit $25K, transfer and gates $750K), all of which is independently useful to patients, staff and rideshare whether or not any fleet ever operates.
7. **It is vertically expandable to six storeys** if evidence later supports it — with no expansion assumed in any economics.

**Not recommended: SA-A.** It fails three independent tests simultaneously. It delivers approximately **140 operational fleet positions, not 300–400** (§3 below). It carries a 1.66× accessory-parking ratio into a discretionary height review. And it requires **medium-voltage service at 3,208 kW**. At $76.3M core cost it is squarely in the $75–100M range the owner set out to avoid, and it does not clear any return test.

**Not recommended as designed: SA-B.** Its logic is right and its stall math is not. Under a literal reading of the 1-per-250-GFA standard it is one stall short of its own requirement — meaning it has no surplus parking to monetise and no deck capacity for staging. S3's $419,265 of annual parking revenue is in direct tension with that (CVR-31 / OQ-26). If the City grants a shared-parking reduction, SA-B becomes viable as a *design*; it still does not become viable as an *investment*.

### 2.3 On the two-site strategy

**Adopt it as a design discipline immediately, and as a capital commitment not at all until a depot site is secured.**

The strategic logic is sound and now quantified. Moving depot functions off South Andrews reduces the electrical service requirement from **3,208 kW to 1,143 kW**, moving the site from medium-voltage primary service to conventional 480 V secondary — worth roughly **$2.0M of avoided utility capital plus about $1.1M of battery** that exists largely to hold the medium-voltage case down. On top of that it avoids housing vehicles at **$544,977 per operational fleet position** on $209/SF healthcare land.

But it is a strategy about a site the owner does not have. **No depot has been identified, priced, zoned-verified, or optioned.** Approximately **$3.15M of SA-B's budget would be stranded** if the depot never materialises. The sequencing rule that follows is the most important operational rule in this plan: **build no mobility-specific infrastructure on South Andrews beyond chargers and the passenger-transfer canopy until the depot-site gate closes.**

## 3. Answers to the questions the assignment posed

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Can South Andrews support 300–400 operational fleet spaces? | **No.** The maximum-fleet scheme yields ~140 after code-required parking (232 stalls), secure-zone boundary loss, turnaround and manoeuvring reserve. Reaching 300 needs ~570 gross stalls — the rejected 7-parking-level rental program |
| What is the minimum to make the site function as a staging ground? | **$4,986,750 excluding land** — demolition, remediation allowance, flood and stormwater work, paving, power, 40 chargers, transfer canopy, streetscape |
| What is the minimum viable development? | **SA-C at $40,898,910 core all-in**, or **SA-C0 at $20,395,184** if no clinical floor is built |
| Does ground-floor med-tail strengthen entitlement, income and story? | **Yes on entitlement and story; only partly on income.** Active frontage is described by S8 p.7 as "the single most important design condition." But med-tail yields on the same expensive podium as everything else |
| Should mobility revenue be underwritten? | **No.** Excluded from every base case per CAN-012. No operator has any documented relationship with this project |
| Is the healthcare thesis correct? | **It is the right thesis and the wrong price.** Healthcare is the most defensible use, the best entitlement story, and the only one that justifies the location. It does not generate enough rent to carry structured parking at Fort Lauderdale construction costs on $209/SF land |

## 4. Maximum rational acquisition price

**Under the development program as modelled: approximately $0–$2 million.**

The residual land analysis returns a positive number in exactly one place — SA-C0 at a 4.0% yield on cost, which supports about **$1.9M**. Every other combination of scenario and yield is negative, ranging to −$32.2M for SA-A at 7.0%.

Three things must be said precisely about this number:

1. **It is not a negotiating position.** It is a finding that this program cannot pay this price.
2. **It does not prove the land is worth $0–2M to anyone else.** The appraisal concluded $11.1M; the prior listing was $12.0M. Another buyer with a different program — particularly one able to reach residential density this program cannot — may rationally pay far more. The gap between $2M and $11M is the value of density and use that a healthcare-and-parking program does not capture.
3. **The most likely competing buyer may be the anchor tenant.** S6 pp.45–46 records that **North Broward Hospital bought both of the closest comparables** — 1404 and 1408 South Andrews — in January and February 2025, one of them under an express condemnation threat. The health system whose expansion underpins the entire demand thesis is an active assembler on this street. That is simultaneously the strongest evidence for the location's healthcare relevance and a warning about who sets the price.

**Recommended transaction posture.** Rather than paying cash for a basis the program cannot support, test structures that convert the land cost from a fixed obligation into a variable one:

- **Seller ground lease** with a participation in project performance
- **Seller carry or seller participation** in a joint venture
- **Long-dated option** to purchase, funded at a fraction of the price, buying time for the entitlement and demand evidence
- **Contribution structure** where a healthcare operator or developer partner brings capital against the land

None of these is documented or negotiated. All of them are cheaper to explore than an $8M cash acquisition into a negative-residual program.

## 7. What evidence would reverse this recommendation

Stated plainly, so that the recommendation is falsifiable:

| Evidence | Threshold that would matter |
|---|---|
| **Health-system anchor** | A master lease or minimum-use guarantee worth **$1.6M–$2.3M per year** of incremental NOI, or an equivalent capital contribution. This is the single most powerful lever, and it is what CAN-013's unvalidated Broward Health spillover thesis would have to become |
| **Public capital** | Grants, TIF, MPO mobility-hub funding, or P3 proceeds covering **$15M–$20M** of development cost (MB-14) |
| **Medical office rents** | Validated NNN rents materially above $50/RSF. Note the requirement is $131 — an incremental rent finding does not rescue this |
| **Land basis** | A basis at **$0–2M**, or a ground lease / participation structure that removes the fixed land obligation |
| **Live Local Act** | If it preempts the 50 du/net acre density cap, the entire alternative-use analysis changes and residential becomes a serious contender (OQ-30 / MB-18) |
| **Construction cost** | GC pricing **30%+ below** the modelled basis. Possible but not plausible at this magnitude |
| **Parking determination** | City acceptance of shared parking would restore SA-B's parking revenue — worth ~45 basis points of yield. Helpful, not decisive |

Absent at least one of the first four, the recommendation stands.

## 8. Decisions required from ownership

1. **Authorise the pre-acquisition diligence programme** (~$100–200K) or decide not to proceed. Everything else waits on this.
2. **Set the underwriting standard** — target yield on cost, hurdle rate, and exit cap basis (MA-06, OQ-18). Without it, "does not clear" is an assertion rather than a measurement.
3. **Decide the transaction posture**: cash acquisition, option, ground lease, participation, or walk away (OQ-20).
4. **Rule on the program basis** — Basis A or Basis B — after test-fit (D-P5 / OQ-14).
5. **Rule on whether SA-C or SA-B is the design target** if the project proceeds.
6. **Rule on the electrical workbook's status** — admit to `sources/` with a provenance note, or reject (OQ-29).
7. **Direct whether alternative uses are to be priced**, particularly SA-D8 (ground lease / partnership) and SA-D1 under a Live Local review.

## 9. How to read the negative finding

This is a recommendation against a **price and a program**, not against the site or the thesis.

The location is genuinely well suited to healthcare-facing mobility. It is a corner parcel with 275 feet of frontage on a hospital corridor, protected open space to the north, ten minutes from downtown, 2.8 miles from the airport, in a Qualified Opportunity Zone, in a district that permits the uses contemplated. The healthcare anchor is the right thesis. The two-site operating logic is correct and quantified. The mobility infrastructure is cheap to provide and useful whether or not a fleet ever arrives.

What the evidence does not support is paying $8 million for the land and then spending $40–86 million on a building whose rents cannot service it. The right response is not to abandon the thesis. It is to **stop treating acquisition as the first step**, close the four cheap gates that could change the answer, and test whether a partner, a public source, or a different transaction structure can carry what the real-estate economics alone cannot.

If none can, the disciplined answer is to walk — and the evidence assembled here is what makes that a defensible decision rather than a lost opportunity.
